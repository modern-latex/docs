## Choosing a language

With babel, the last language named is the one the document is in; the ones before it are languages it quotes:

```latex
\usepackage[english,russian]{babel}
\begin{document}
\today                          % 28 сентября 2026 г.
\foreignlanguage{english}{A phrase in English.}
\end{document}
```

`\selectlanguage`, `\foreignlanguage`, `otherlanguage`, `otherlanguage*` and `hyphenrules` switch languages, and `main=` names the main one explicitly.

polyglossia names languages by command and gives each one a command and an environment of its own:

```latex
\usepackage{polyglossia}
\setmainlanguage{english}
\setotherlanguage{french}
\textfrench{Une phrase en français.}
\begin{french} ... \end{french}
```

## What a language changes

* **Hyphenation.** Words break by that language's own TeX patterns, no closer to their ends than the language allows. Patterns for 78 languages ship with mLaTeX and are read the first time a language is chosen; English is built in.
* **Captions and dates.** `\abstractname`, `\figurename`, `\refname`, `\contentsname`, `\today` and the rest are in the language, for 26 languages: English, German, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Brazilian Portuguese, Dutch, Russian, Ukrainian, Bulgarian, Polish, Czech, Croatian, Hungarian, Romanian, Greek, Turkish, Swedish, Danish, Norwegian, Finnish, Catalan, Arabic, Hebrew and Persian.
* **Direction.** Arabic, Hebrew and Persian paragraphs read right to left.
* **Typography.** French sets a thin unbreakable space before `;`, `!`, `?` and `»` and after `«`, and a word space before `:`. Arabic, Persian and Urdu use their own digits.

## Right-to-left text

Arabic, Hebrew and Persian paragraphs start at the right, with indents, list margins and labels on the right and heading numbers at their title's right. Each line is ordered by Unicode's bidirectional algorithm, so a number or a Latin phrase inside Arabic keeps its own order, and Arabic inside an English paragraph reads correctly.

```latex
\usepackage{polyglossia}
\setmainlanguage{english}
\setotherlanguage{arabic}
\begin{Arabic}
مرحبا بالعالم
\end{Arabic}
```

Boxes, minipages and tables follow the direction of their text, and a table in Arabic or Hebrew runs right to left. Justified Arabic is stretched with kashida, lengthening one join in a word, rather than only widening spaces. `bidi`'s `\setRTL` and `\setLTR` set the direction directly.

Arabic, Persian and Urdu are drawn with Noto Naskh Arabic, which mLaTeX carries, so an Arabic document looks the same on every system. polyglossia's `numerals=maghrib` keeps the digits as typed.

## Greek, Cyrillic and other scripts

New Computer Modern, which mLaTeX carries, draws Greek, Cyrillic, Hebrew, Armenian, Georgian and Devanagari in the same design as the Latin text, in every cut: bold, italic, sans and typewriter. Accented Latin and Cyrillic words hyphenate letter by letter.

## Chinese, Japanese and Korean

These are drawn with your system's fonts, since mLaTeX carries none for them. A line may break between any two characters, with the kinsoku rules respected: never before a closing mark, a small kana or a long vowel, and never after an opening bracket. A run of Latin text among them is kept whole.

```latex
\usepackage{CJK}
\begin{CJK}{UTF8}{gbsn}
中文文本可以在任意两个字之间换行。
\end{CJK}
```

`CJK` and `CJKutf8`'s block is read; its encoding and font family are accepted, and the characters come from the system.
